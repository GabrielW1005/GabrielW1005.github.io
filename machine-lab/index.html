Work, force and motion

Work is energy transferred by a force. An ideal machine trades force for distance; it does not create energy.

F₁d₁ = F₂d₂
Ideal MA = d₁/d₂ = F₂/F₁ at balance

Ideal balance and steady motion: with no resistance, the equation compares input work and output work. At rest, actual work is zero; compare the distances the machine would move.

Balancing masses: m₁g d₁ = m₂g d₂ → m₁d₁ = m₂d₂. The same gravitational acceleration g cancels. The moving load’s acceleration is not g and does not generally cancel.

Accelerating: Fnet = ma. Net work changes kinetic energy. The energy line includes extra work from your pull and energy absorbed at travel stops. The force–distance plot compares the regular input force and load weight; its shaded areas show their work. Extra pointer work appears separately in the energy line.

Pulling: a virtual grip first supports the load, like holding it by hand. Dragging stretches its spring and adds force; a damping term responds to relative speed. The force is capped at 1000 N and limited where needed to keep ropes taut. Release to remove it. The cursor never teleports a weight.

Resize: mass handles snap in 0.5 kg steps; dimension handles use the increments in Settings. Horizontal ramp extension uses 0.1 m steps. You can also type a value. Computed balance values stay exact internally; ≈ marks a rounded display. Drawn area is proportional to mass (twice the mass means twice the area). Input mass is 0–200 kg; output is 1–200 kg. Resizing or changing dimensions starts a fresh energy experiment.

Distances: trajectories show actual body paths. The work comparison uses distance along the relevant force: vertical rise/fall for gravity. A ramp’s box follows the slope; a lever endpoint follows an arc. These are different from their vertical work distances. Path length totals keep growing after a reversal; displacement can reverse.

Vectors: arrows label gravity, applied force and pointer force. Supports and internal forces are not all drawn. Force arrows share an automatic scale, so their lengths show their relative sizes. Acceleration arrows use their own shared scale and a separate toggle. Lever acceleration arrows include acceleration toward the pivot; the numeric readout shows acceleration along the path. On a ramp, the acceleration question is along the slope. Lever acceleration questions refer to the instant the lever is horizontal.

The engine: constrained Newtonian differential equations. Undamped motion uses small velocity-Verlet steps. With settling resistance, linear motion uses exact damped solutions and the lever uses fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps. Ropes and machine parts are massless. The friction / drag slider starts at zero (ideal) and keeps your setting when you switch problems, machines or modes. Zero means no resistance during free motion. A nonzero setting adds speed-dependent resistance; motion energy becomes heat. Loads follow each machine’s constrained paths; independent pendulum motion is not modeled. Syringes use incompressible fluid and neglect fluid inertia and pressure-height differences. These are ideal models, not a general collision sandbox.

Adjustable friction / drag: Off is ideal; Light gives gentle slowing; Medium and Strong remove motion faster. This models viscous drag, not dry surface friction: the resistance opposes motion and is zero at rest. You can change it while running without resetting the machine. Its damping rate ranges from 0 to 3 per second and may be reduced to keep ropes taut. Friction slows motion; it does not pull the machine back toward the center.

Balance is not a return force: gravity pulls on both lever masses. When their weight × lever arm products match, tilting the beam shortens both horizontal lever arms by the same factor, so their turning effects still cancel. These balanced machines have no preferred center. With resistance off, a moving release keeps moving; a release from rest stays at that position. A spring or a center of mass below a pivot can create a restoring force, but neither is part of these models. Hanging weights do not swing independently here. Travel stops absorb collision energy; elastic bouncing is not modeled. Optional resistance brings motion to rest and records the energy as heat. Run remains active.

Minimum force: lifting questions ask for the smallest whole-number force in newtons that starts upward motion from rest. First find the balance force, then choose the next larger whole number. The exact balance force does not start motion.

Distance tests: a bounded actuator moves the input through the distance you enter. Its force and work are included in the simulation. A wrong distance produces a correspondingly wrong output rise.

Adaptive practice: each machine includes force, mass, distance and acceleration problems across progressive stages. Correct answers advance that skill; repeated mistakes generate another problem at an easier stage. Master all four skills to move to the next machine.
