Trade distance for force.

Work is energy transferred by a force. Conservation of energy means energy cannot be created or destroyed. An ideal machine trades force for distance; it does not create energy.

For an ideal machine moving steadily, input work equals output work:

F₁d₁ = F₂d₂
MA = F₂ / F₁ = d₁ / d₂

Moving the input farther lets you use a smaller input force. The blue and orange paths show the two motions. Live bars compare displacement magnitudes along the forces using the same scale. They measure from the reset position; reversing motion can make them shrink. The graph shows signed displacement. On a ramp, the box follows the slope, but output work uses its vertical rise.

Balance: equal opposing turning effects or forces mean no acceleration. At rest, no work is done. The exact balance threshold does not start a stationary load moving. A slightly larger force lifts it.

Moving: net work changes kinetic energy. These models calculate acceleration from the connected masses and machine constraints. When the mechanism reaches a travel stop, its motion stops and the lost kinetic energy is accounted for as energy at the stop.

Use the playground: drag weights onto blue or orange pans, drag round handles, or enter exact dimensions. Select “Free play” for unrestricted experimenting. “Auto balance” changes the input mass or geometry to restore balance.

For vertical lever forces, work uses vertical rise/fall, not the curved arc length. In gears and wheels, a point on a rim follows a circle while a hanging weight travels vertically; the rope travel equals the appropriate radius × rotation angle.

The geared winch is a compound machine; syringes are a related hydraulic machine. Hydraulic pressure-height differences and fluid inertia are ignored. Real friction would require more input work.
