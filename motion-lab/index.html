How Motion Lab works

Controls

Hold ← / → to drive the wheels and ↓ to brake. Release to coast. Hold J / L to lower or raise motor strength smoothly at 0.50 m/s² per simulated second while running, or per real second while paused. The strength slider and J/L buttons work too. Press Space to pause and R to reset.

Four directional thrusters

Use the Thrusters switch to show or hide the jet modules. Hold W up, A left, S down, D right to apply thrust in fixed screen directions. The flame points opposite the force. Multiple jets can fire together. Motor strength affects only the wheels; thruster force is adjusted separately in Course & forces and is preset in challenges.

The car has a fixed mass of 100 kg. Thrust is a force measured in newtons (N). Each jet contributes acceleration F/m, in m/s². For example, 1200 N supplies 12.00 m/s² in its direction. Gravity is added to that acceleration: upward thrust must exceed 981 N to lift this car from level ground on Earth. Jet modules are ideal, stabilized actuators with no torque or fuel-mass change.

Graph challenges

There are 28 challenges in two sets: Motor graphs and Thrusters & gravity. The first challenge in each set is available immediately. Settings, motor strength, and any starting velocity are loaded automatically. Thruster challenges disable the wheel motor. Follow the shaded phases and dashed example. Scores measure graph shape with timing tolerance; prescribed thruster phases also require the indicated jet.

Choose an unlock score from 90–100% (default 90%). Every completed attempt displays its percentage, including a 0% attempt, in the header even when minimized. Reset keeps the previous score visible until a new attempt finishes. Minimize saves play space; Expand restores instructions and settings. Scores and unlocks last for the open page; the selected percentage is remembered on this device when storage is available.

Gravity and curved graphs

The gravity panel beside the graphs always shows the current setting: Earth 9.81, Mars 3.71, Moon 1.62, or zero gravity, in m/s². Some explicitly labeled gravity-lab challenges switch or gradually vary g. These are artificial experiments, not instantaneous trips between planets; their target graphs use the same changing gravity as your car.

Distance concave up: speed increases. Distance concave down: speed decreases. Speed concave up/down: the rate of change of speed increases/decreases. A falling car can have constant speed if upward thrust balances gravity. An upward force can slow a falling car before reversing it.

Contact, flight, and impacts

The car is an ideal point mass moving in two dimensions. On a road, the motor and brake act along the road, and gravity and thrust are projected along its slope. The road supplies the normal force needed to follow its curvature. If that would require the road to pull the car inward, the car leaves the surface. Turning off airborne motion creates an ideal track guide; enabling thrusters turns airborne motion back on.

In free flight, aₓ = Fₓ/m and aᵧ = Fᵧ/m − g. Wheels cannot push or brake in the air. Landings remove velocity into the surface and retain tangential velocity. A pit wall stops velocity into the wall while the car can slide down, thrust away, or thrust upward. The camera follows missed jumps until the car falls below −24 m. With jets disabled and zero gravity, a missed jump ends after three seconds. All runs end by 120 simulated seconds.

There is no air drag, rolling resistance, suspension, wheel inertia, fuel depletion, or bounce. Car tilt and wheel spin are decorative. Impacts are ideal instantaneous changes of velocity; their impulses are not shown as finite acceleration readings.

Units and measurements

Distance (m) is the entire traveled path, including flight and reversals; it never decreases. Speed (m/s) is √(vₓ² + vᵧ²). Horizontal velocity is positive rightward; vertical velocity is positive upward. Acceleration size (m/s²) is √(aₓ² + aᵧ²), including changes in direction. In unpowered free flight it equals g. Time is in seconds on the horizontal axis of both graphs.

The distance graph's slope is speed; the area under the speed graph is distance. Both graphs and all readouts use the same simulated state. A fixed 1/240 s physics step, Runge–Kutta road motion, constant-acceleration flight steps, and within-step contact events keep the numerical model consistent. Playback speed changes the viewing rate only.
