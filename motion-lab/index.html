The physics behind the drive

The car is an ideal cart constrained to a smooth track. It always follows the road, even at high speeds. There are no jumps, collisions, air resistance, or wheel rotational inertia.

What makes it accelerate?

Along the track, a = amotor − g sin(θ) + abrake. The angle θ is positive when the track rises to the right. Gravity accelerates the car downhill; it can slow an uphill car until it reverses. Releasing the motor applies no motor force.

The brake opposes motion at up to 6 m/s². Once stopped, it holds the car if it can balance the other forces. The car's mass cancels because the controls specify acceleration (force per unit mass).

Read the graphs

  • Distance is the total path length traveled, including up and down ramps. It never decreases, even when the car reverses.
  • Speed is the magnitude of velocity and is never negative. The slope of the distance graph is speed; the area under the speed graph is distance.
  • Track velocity is positive toward the right and negative toward the left. Track acceleration is the signed change of that velocity. On a curve, the car also has acceleration due to changing direction.
  • On the speed graph, a positive slope means speeding up and a negative slope means slowing down. That slope is not always the signed track acceleration.

Controls

Hold ← / A or → / D to apply the motor. Hold ↓ / S to brake. Space runs or pauses; R resets. The buttons also work by touch. When paused, Step advances 0.1 simulated seconds. Playback speed changes viewing speed, not the physics.

Changing the track, height, gravity, or initial velocity resets the run. Motor acceleration can be changed during a run. A run pauses after 120 simulated seconds so graphs stay readable.

Integrated in fixed 1/240 s steps using fourth-order Runge–Kutta, with stops and reversals resolved within a step. Wheel rotation is visual only. Read about gravity on an incline.
